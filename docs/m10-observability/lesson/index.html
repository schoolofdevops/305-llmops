Observability — Metrics, Traces & the Cost of Tokens

Time

Lesson video ~35 min · Lab ~60 min hands-on, depending on your machine.

Let me set up the Monday this module is built for. Three things arrive on you before lunch. A product manager says OpsMate "feels slow this week" and wants to know if that is real. Finance forwards the model-serving bill and asks why it went up thirty percent when traffic barely moved. And there is an incident review at two, for a request that took eleven seconds, where someone will ask "where did the time go?" and expect a real answer. Three questions, three different people. And right now you cannot answer any of them with evidence. You can feel the system. You cannot see it.

That is the gap this module closes. And here is the catch, one that creates issues for engineers who are comfortable with ordinary web observability. For a normal web service, "latency" is basically one number, that is the time from request to response, and a single P50 on a dashboard tells you most of what you need. A model service breaks that. A request to OpsMate has two phases with two different latencies (you met them in M1, that is prefill and decode), and its cost is counted in tokens, not requests. Watch a model service the way you watch a web service and you will average away exactly the thing you needed to see. So the real work of this module is learning what to measure for a service whose "slow" is always one of two different slows, and whose "expensive" is counted per token.

Two instruments, two questions — metrics vs traces​

Before the mechanics, keep this picture in mind, because it holds everything that follows together. A hospital watches a patient with two very different instruments, and it needs both.

There is the ward chart, that is the vitals clipped to the end of the bed. Heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, sampled every few minutes and plotted as lines. It tells you the patient's state over time, and it covers every patient on the ward at once. If the whole ward's temperature is creeping up, the chart would show it. What the chart cannot tell you is the story of one bad afternoon.

For that there is the case file, that is one patient's full record of a single episode. Admitted at 2:04, bloods drawn at 2:11, seen by the consultant at 2:40, sent to imaging at 3:05. It is one journey, every step timestamped, so you can see exactly where the delay was. What the case file cannot tell you is whether the ward as a whole is trending badly. It is one patient, one episode.

Observability for a model service is exactly these two instruments:

  • Metrics are the ward chart. Numbers sampled over time, that is tokens per second, queue depth, requests in flight, across the whole service. They answer "is it slow, and getting slower?" and "is the queue building?" This is Prometheus and Grafana.
  • Traces are the case file. One request's full journey, that is retrieve took 40ms, generate took 9 seconds, it cost 500 tokens, every step on a timeline. They answer "where did THIS request spend its eleven seconds?" This is OpenTelemetry and Phoenix.
Analogy, stated plainly

Metrics = the ward chart: vitals over time, every patient, sampled. "Is the service slow and is the queue growing?" Traces = the case file: one request, every step, timestamped. "Where did this specific request spend its time, and what did it cost?" You need both, and neither substitutes for the other. A dashboard cannot tell you why one request was slow; a single trace cannot tell you the service is trending badly.

The metric model — M1's vocabulary, now as production signals​

You already have the words for the ward chart. In M1 you met the two phases and their two latencies, and in M3 you watched them on the engine's own /metrics. M10 does not re-teach them. It puts them into production: the same signals, now scraped by a Prometheus that lives in the cluster and read off a shared wall instead of a laptop curl.

Here is the mapping you carry forward. Every one of these was a concept in M1. Now it is a production signal you set alerts on.

Read the split the way M1 taught it. TTFT is mostly about prompt length. A long prompt is a long prefill, that is a long wait before the first word. ITL is mostly about answer length, that is the steady rhythm as tokens come out (M1 called it ITL — inter-token latency — and mentioned TPOT as the older name for the same idea). When the PM says "it feels slow", your first question is no longer "how slow". It is "slow to start or slow to finish?", because those are two different phases with two different fixes. The prompt-tokens rate and the decode-tokens rate are the two panels that answer it.

And there is a third signal that is neither phase. It is load. requests_processing is how many requests the engine is working on right now. requests_deferred is how many are waiting for a slot. When OpsMate "feels slow" and both phase rates look fine, the deferred gauge is usually the answer: the request is not slow, it is waiting. That difference, slow versus waiting, is invisible to a single latency number, and obvious the moment you have the deferred panel. It is also the exact signal an autoscaler will watch in M11, so you are meeting it here at the source.

SLOs per phase — why one P50 is a lie for a model service​

Now here is the important part, the sharpest idea in the module, and the one most likely to be doing quiet damage in a real setup. When you set a latency SLO for a model service as a single number, say "P50 under 2 seconds", you are averaging two phases together, and the average hides both.

Think about how you would judge a restaurant. You do not judge it on one number, the time from walking in to walking out, because that blends two very different things: how long the kitchen took to cook, and how long the waiter took to bring it. A slow kitchen and a slow waiter are different problems with different fixes. And a single door-to-door average lets a restaurant with a fast kitchen and a hopeless waiter look the same as one with the opposite problem. You measure kitchen time and waiter time separately, or you cannot fix either.

Prefill and decode are the kitchen and the waiter. Prefill (TTFT) is one SLO. Decode (ITL, or end-to-end for a given answer length) is another. Set them separately, or you are blind on both.

Here is the P50 lie made concrete, and you will make it real in the lab. Lets say OpsMate serves a mix of traffic: lots of short questions ("what's the rollback command?") and a few long ones (a senior engineer pasting a two thousand token incident timeline and asking for analysis). The short ones are fast. The long ones are slow. Your overall P50 would sit comfortably under target, because half your requests are the short ones, and the median falls among them. The dashboard is green. Meanwhile every long request, the ones your most important users send, is blowing the SLO, and the P50 cannot see them because they are up in the tail. A single median on mixed traffic is not a summary. It is a hiding place. The fix is to read latency per phase and per traffic class, not as one blended number. In the lab you will drive exactly this mixed load, watch the overall P50 stay green, and then read the per-phase signals that show the long requests suffering.

Before the lab, feel the lie yourself. In the simulator below, OpsMate's traffic is two populations: fast short-answer requests and slow long-answer requests, forming two humps on the latency histogram. Drag the traffic mix and switch which single number the dashboard reports. Watch the mean and P50 slide into the empty valley between the humps, describing almost no real request, while P95 and P99 stay out on the slow hump where your important users actually are. Shift the mix and the average moves even though no request got any faster.

The habit to carry

Never set a model-service latency SLO as one number. Split it: a TTFT target (the prompt-reading phase) and an ITL-or-end-to-end target (the answer-writing phase). Then watch the tail, not the median, on mixed traffic — the P50 is where slow long-requests go to hide. "The dashboard is green" and "every important request is slow" are routinely true at the same time.

Scrape mechanics on Kubernetes — briefly, because the LLM part is which families​

You know Prometheus exists, and you know the pull model: a scraper polls each target's /metrics on an interval and stores the samples. On the cluster, M10's Prometheus is one pod with a ConfigMap that lists one target, that is the model server's Service opsmate-model:8080, scraped every 5 seconds. That is the whole mechanism, and it is not new to you, so the module spends its attention on the part that is LLM-specific: which metric families matter, and what each one answers. A basic Prometheus tutorial will show you up and http_requests_total. Those tell you the engine is alive and how many HTTP calls it took. Useful, but they do not tell you one thing about whether the model is healthy. The families that matter for a model server are the token rates, the phase timings, and the queue gauges above, the ones a basic dashboard does not know to graph. Knowing to reach for requests_deferred instead of http_requests_total is the skill. Standing up Prometheus is not.

Dashboards as shared truth — the Grafana dashboard​

A metric no one looks at is not observability, it is storage. The dashboard is where a metric becomes a shared fact, the thing the PM, finance, and the on-call engineer all point at during that Monday, instead of three people trading stories. That is why the module ships one Grafana dashboard as a real artifact, checked into the manifest, not clicked together by hand: it is the OpsMate model server's vital signs on one wall, and it comes up the same for everyone who applies the file.

The dashboard is the M1 metric model made visible: decode throughput, prompt-processing rate, requests processing, requests deferred, and the lifetime token counters. Each panel carries a description that says what it shows and why it is the signal. In the lab you will port-forward to it, drive the M3 load test, and watch the wall move: the deferred gauge would climb as concurrency rises, while decode tok/s holds steady because the engine is batching. Reading that pattern live, that is queue forming while throughput holds, is the difference between knowing the words and knowing the system.

Tracing the pipeline — the ask → retrieve → generate waterfall​

Now the case file. A metric tells you the service is slow. It cannot tell you where in one request the time went. For that you need a trace, that is the request broken into spans. Each span is a step with a start, a duration, and attributes, nested to show what called what. OpsMate's /ask is a three-step pipeline (you built it in M4, that is retrieve, then generate), so its trace is three spans:

Read the waterfall and the eleven-second incident answers itself. The retrieve span is 40 milliseconds, so retrieval was not the problem. The generate span is nine seconds and holds 500 tokens, so the time and the cost are both in the model call, and now you can say so with evidence instead of guessing. That is the case file doing its job: one request, every step, the delay located.

Two honest notes about the instrumentation, because this is a fast-moving area and the module will not pretend otherwise. First, the span kinds and attribute names OpsMate uses (CHAIN, RETRIEVER, LLM, llm.token_count.*, input.value) follow the OpenInference conventions that Phoenix reads. Second, there is a parallel effort, the OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions, which is standardising these names across the whole industry, and it is still changing: names shift between versions. The engineering answer to a moving standard is not to avoid it. It is to pin the versions you build against, so your traces do not silently change form under you. That is exactly what the app's requirements.txt does. The Deep Dive walks the semconv status and the pinning strategy in full.

Tokens as cost per request — the bill, on the span​

Here is where the trace answers finance's question, not just the incident review's. Every model response comes back with a usage block, that is prompt_tokens, completion_tokens, total_tokens, which is the engine's own count of the tokens that request burned. The app lifts those numbers straight onto the generate span as llm.token_count.* attributes. So every trace does not just show where the time went. It shows what the request cost, in the only unit that matters for a model bill: tokens.

That is the whole chain from a running request to the invoice. A request comes in. The model returns its usage. The app puts the usage on the span. The trace store holds it. And a query summing llm.token_count.total across a day's traces is your token bill, broken down however you sliced the spans, that is by user, by endpoint, by prompt length. When finance asks why the bill went up thirty percent, you do not guess. You group the traces and show them: the long-prompt requests doubled, and long prompts are expensive because prefill reads every token. The Deep Dive builds this aggregation query step by step. Keep this in mind for now: cost stops being a monthly surprise and becomes a per-request attribute you can see the moment it happens.

Self-hosted vs managed — the worksheet, not a verdict​

Once you can see the per-request token cost, the natural next question is whether self-hosting was worth it at all — a hosted API charges by the token with no box to run, so why carry the Kubernetes, the observability stack, the on-call load? There is no universal answer, because the two paths trade a fixed cost for a variable one, and which wins depends on your volume. Renting a car by the day is cheap for a weekend trip and expensive for a year; owning one is the reverse. Self-hosting is the owned car: a fixed cost (the box, and your time operating it) that gets cheaper per trip the more you drive it. A hosted API is the rental: no fixed cost, but every trip is billed.

The formula is one line, and every number on the self-hosted side of it is one you already measured in this course — nothing here is invented, and nothing requires a paid API call:

self-hosted cost per 1M tokens ≈ (box cost, amortized over its useful life) ÷ (tokens the box serves in that life)

Fill in the box's cost and lifetime yourself — a real hardware price or a real cloud instance rate you look up (this course made no cloud spend, so it has no captured number for that half). For the throughput half, you already have it: M2 measured ~180 tok/s decode for the Q4 model on this course's laptop-class CPU path, and this module's own load test (Step 5, the M3 load test replayed against the cluster) measured the same engine's real behavior under concurrent load — p50/p95 climbing from ~3.8s/6.4s at concurrency 4 to ~13.9s/18.5s at concurrency 16, the queueing curve, not a flat rate. Multiply your box's sustained tokens-per-second by its uptime to get "tokens served in its life," and you have the self-hosted side of the formula filled with real numbers.

The hosted side is one lookup: today's published $-per-million-tokens on the provider's pricing page for a comparably-capable model. Put the two numbers side by side and you have an answer for your volume — not a verdict this course can hand you, because "self-hosted is cheaper" and "hosted is cheaper" are both true, at different request volumes, for the same model. What this course gives you is the discipline to run the comparison with real measured numbers instead of a vendor's marketing page or a gut feeling, the same evidence-over-configuration habit from every other module.

What not to log — the receptionist's register​

There is a sharp edge on all of this, and it is why the log-hygiene beat is in the module and not a footnote. Look again at that ask span: its input.value attribute is the user's question, in full. That is on purpose, because you often need the input to debug a bad answer. But sit with what it means. Every trace now holds the exact text a user typed, stored in your trace backend, visible to anyone who can open Phoenix. For OpsMate that text is incident details. For a medical or legal or HR assistant it is far worse.

Think of the receptionist's register at a doctor's office. It is right and useful for it to record that a patient arrived at 2:04 and saw Dr. Rao, that is the timing, the flow, the fact of the visit. It must not record what the patient whispered to the doctor behind the closed door. The register is for the journey, not the confession. A trace is that register: recording that a request retrieved three documents and took nine seconds is exactly what you want. Recording the full sensitive prompt text as a permanent attribute is the whispered confession ending up in the register.

So logging a prompt into a trace is a decision, not a default. In the lab you will see your own question sitting in a Phoenix span, and then make the decision honestly: keep it (you are debugging, the data is not sensitive, access is controlled) or redact it (drop the attribute before the span is exported, so the form of the request is traced but the content is not). Both are fine. Choosing blindly is not. This is the front edge of the guardrails work in M12, which handles the harder version, that is PII detection and prompt-injection filtering, at the gateway. M10's job is to make you see the sensitive data in your own telemetry and decide, instead of discovering it in a breach report.

The decision, stated plainly

Traces carry prompt text by default (input.value). That is useful for debugging and dangerous for privacy — the two are the same feature. Decide per deployment: is this input safe to store, given who can read the trace backend? If not, redact the attribute at instrumentation time. Never let "it was the default" be the reason a user's sensitive text is sitting in your trace store. M12 automates the harder cases; M10 makes you see it and choose.

One more thing the lab will throw at you — the service-link crash​

The KIND phase of this course has a running pattern: each module ships one real, reproducible failure you fix live, because fixing a real crash teaches a mechanism that a paragraph cannot. M8 was the hostAliases IP. M9 was the wrong kit filename. M10 is the service-link env crash, and here is the mechanism so you recognise it when the lab hits it.

Kubernetes has an old convenience feature. For every Service, it injects environment variables into pods named after that Service, that is <NAME>_PORT, <NAME>_SERVICE_HOST, and so on, so older apps could find services through the environment. The observability stack has a Service named phoenix. So Kubernetes would inject PHOENIX_PORT=tcp://10.96.x.x:6006 into the Phoenix pod. And Phoenix also reads an env var called PHOENIX_PORT, as its own listen port, expecting a plain integer. It gets tcp://10.96.x.x:6006, cannot read it as a number, and crashes at startup with a log line that names the fix. The lab applies Phoenix as shipped, you read that crash log, and you fix it, either by setting PHOENIX_PORT explicitly so your value wins, or by turning the injection off with enableServiceLinks: false. Meeting a real service-link collision once is worth more than reading that the feature exists, because now you would recognise the whole class of "why is this env var set to a tcp:// string?" bugs on sight.

Where this leaves OpsMate — and the hand-off to M11​

By the end of this module OpsMate is observable: metrics on a shared wall, traces of every request, cost visible per request, and a clear policy on what its telemetry is allowed to store. That is OpsMate v2.5, part one, the platform you can finally see.

And it sets up the next module directly, because of one rule that observability makes obvious: you cannot autoscale what you cannot measure. The requests_deferred gauge you learn to read here is the exact signal an autoscaler acts on in M11. When the queue builds, add an engine. When it drains, remove one. Autoscaling is not a new idea bolted on. It is a control loop wrapped around the metric you are about to start watching. You measure first. Then you let the measurement drive the system. See you in the lab.
