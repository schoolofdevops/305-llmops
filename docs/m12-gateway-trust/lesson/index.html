AI Gateway, Eval Gates & Guardrails

Time

Lesson video ~45 min · Lab ~75 min hands-on, depending on your machine. There is a safe stopping point mid-lab.

OpsMate works. It is autoscaled, promoted through git, observable. And it has no front door.

Here is what that costs you, told as three ordinary weeks on call. The first week, someone on the data team wired OpsMate into a nightly job that re-summarized every ticket. It ran an infinite retry loop over a weekend. Nobody noticed until the model host was pinned flat on Monday, and when finance asked "who was that," the honest answer was "we can't tell — every request came from the same shared credential." The second week, a candidate model got promoted — the exact PR-driven promotion you built in M11 — and it was worse than the one it replaced, quietly, on the kind of questions that matter during an incident. No test stood between the merge and production, so the regression shipped and the first person to find it was an engineer trusting a bad answer at the worst possible moment. The third week, a support transcript with a customer's name and phone number went into a prompt, through the model, and out into a log that six teams can read. That is a data-protection incident with a paper trail, and it started as a helpful feature.

None of those three is a model-quality problem. The model was fine. What was missing is the thing that stands between the model working and the model being a product you can operate: a controlled entry point where you know who is asking, cap what they can spend, test what you promote, and scrub what goes in and comes out. That is trust, and trust is built at the door. This module builds the door.

Resource path — 8 GB core runs everything required

This is a Compose-phase module: the KIND cluster from M8–M11 comes down first (one heavyweight stack at a time), and the gateway stack comes up in its place. The stack is LiteLLM + Postgres + two Presidio services (analyzer + anonymizer), fronting host Ollama — about 1.9 GiB steady on the 8 GB path. Everything required runs here. Two things are honest scope notes, not omissions: the eval gate is authored as real CI (a GitHub Actions workflow and an Argo Workflows step) but run as a local dry-run against the gateway — a full in-cluster replay is the 16 GB / cluster path, called out where it applies. And the RAG-metrics library ragas is surveyed, not installed; the golden-set judge stays the M5 two-layer pattern that already runs on this box.

You already run gateways. An API gateway in front of a REST service — auth, rate limits, routing — is not new to this audience. So this lesson does not re-teach the gateway pattern. It teaches the parts that are different because the thing behind the door is a language model: the cost of a call is unpredictable and includes tokens you cannot see, the "test suite" is a set of graded judgments rather than green/red asserts, and the dangerous inputs are sentences in English, not malformed JSON. Every section below is an LLM-specific delta on machinery you already know.

The gateway is a hotel front desk — one door, keys, tabs, house rules​

Start with the picture, because it organizes everything else in the module.

Think of a hotel with one front desk. Guests do not each hold a master key to the whole building; they check in at the desk and get a keycard that opens their room and nothing else, tied to their name, with a spending limit on the minibar. There is one door everyone comes through, the desk knows who each guest is, every charge goes on that guest's tab, and the house rules — no smoking, quiet after eleven — are enforced at the desk, not renegotiated room by room. The desk is not the hotel. It is the single point where identity, spending, and rules are applied consistently.

The gateway is that front desk for OpsMate. Every client comes through one endpoint (/v1, OpenAI-compatible, so no client code changes — the same discipline you have kept since M2). The desk hands out virtual keys instead of the master credential. Each key carries a budget. House rules — caching, fallback, PII masking, injection checks — are applied at the desk to everything that passes through. In this module the desk is LiteLLM, backed by Postgres (the desk needs a ledger to remember keys and their running tabs).

Read the order in that diagram, because the order is the lesson. A request is authenticated to a key, then the pre-call guardrails run before the model sees anything, then it is routed (with cache and fallback in the path), and only then does spend get recorded against the key's tab. Mask-then-model, not model-then-mask. Everything in this lesson is one of those boxes.

Virtual keys are room keycards — identity and a limit, not a master key​

The first job of the desk is to stop handing out the master key.

Right now, every OpsMate client uses the same credential — like giving every guest a master key to the whole hotel. When the nightly job loops, you cannot tell it apart from a human on call, cannot cap it separately, cannot revoke it without revoking everyone. A virtual key fixes all three at once: it is a room keycard. You mint one per consumer (on-call, nightly-summarizer, eval-runner), each with an alias so spend is attributable and a max budget so a runaway consumer trips its own limit instead of the whole floor's. Revoke one key and only that consumer is locked out.

Mechanically this is why the desk needs Postgres. The master key generates virtual keys through /key/generate; each key is a row in the datastore with its alias, its budget, and its running spend. That row is the tab. No datastore, no per-key tab, no budget enforcement — which is exactly why the smoke test proved the gateway needs the DB before anything else.

The $0-cost lie — an open-bar tab nobody prices​

Here is the trap that catches everyone the first time, and it is specific to self-hosting.

You mint a key with a tight budget, point three requests at it, and check the tab. It reads 0.0. Not "small" — zero. You could loop that key a million times and the budget gate would never fire. The reason is that a self-hosted model is free by default: LiteLLM has a price list for the commercial providers, but your Ollama model isn't on it, so every call is priced at nothing. The budget is real; the bill it checks against is empty. It is an open-bar tab — the guest is drinking, but nobody wrote a price next to the drinks, so the total stays zero and the limit is meaningless.

The fix is to price your own infrastructure. You add input_cost_per_token and output_cost_per_token to the model config — illustrative rates that stand in for the real cost of the CPU-seconds and memory that call consumed. Now each request has a non-zero cost, the tab accrues, and a tiny-budget key trips on the second call with Budget has been exceeded!. The lesson to carry off this box: a budget without a price is decoration. On managed models the provider prices for you; on your own infra, if you do not set the price, the gate you think you have does not exist.

Feel all three of these at once before you wire them. The simulator below mints the same three keys you will, streams a scripted week that includes the batch job's retry-loop burst day, and lets you set the price. Set it to $0 and replay: the burst is invisible, every tab stays at $0.00. Set a real price and replay: the batch key trips its budget and its later calls bounce at the door — while the interactive assistant finishes the week untouched. Then send a 10-turn chat and watch resent history make each turn cost more than the last.

🕹 The Spend Gate — Keys, Budgets, and the $0 LieOpen in new tab ↗
Self-hosted spend is zero until you price it

A max_budget on a virtual key does nothing while the model behind it costs 0.0 per token — the default for any self-hosted model. Set input_cost_per_token / output_cost_per_token on the model (a stand-in for real infra cost) or your budgets are cosmetic. Verify it the way the lab does: three calls, then read the tab — a still-zero tab means the price is missing, not that the traffic was free.

The tokens you get billed for but never see​

And there is a second bite hiding inside the first, straight out of M2. Qwen3 is a thinking model: it generates hidden reasoning tokens before it writes the visible answer. You met this in M2 as compatibility drift — the /v1 chat route does not honor a think:false request the way /api/generate does, so it silently generates reasoning tokens and the usage meter counts them. There, "the largest planet is Jupiter" billed ~175 completion tokens against a dozen visible words. At the gateway the same accounting applies to every backend behind the door: in the M12 spike, "GATEWAY OK" — three visible words — billed 123 tokens, because the model thought its way there first. Cap max_tokens low and you can get a response with no visible text at all whose completion-token count is the full cap: the model spent its whole budget thinking and never got to the answer.

This is not a bug to fix; it is a cost you must account for. The keycard tab has to include the minibar charges the guest never sees on the shelf. When you set budgets, size them for the tokens the model thinks, not just the tokens it says — the Deep Dive walks the exact counting path from request to per-key spend row.

Caching is the barista remembering the last order​

Not every request should reach the model. Some are repeats.

A good barista, when the same person orders the same drink they ordered two minutes ago, does not re-derive the recipe from scratch — they remember and pour. Caching is that. The gateway keeps the last answer for an identical request and, on an exact repeat, returns the same response without touching the model — in the spike, the same response id came back in ~0.08 s where the cold call took ~4 s, and the repeat shows up in the spend log tagged cache_hit. On the 8 GB path the cache is local (in-process, no Redis).

The LLM-specific extension worth naming: semantic caching. Exact-match only catches character-for-character repeats, but two on-call engineers will phrase the same question differently ("payments api throwing 500s" vs "payments-api is returning 5xx"). Semantic caching embeds the request — reusing exactly the vector machinery from M4 — and treats requests whose embeddings are close enough as the same order. It is more powerful and more dangerous: set the similarity threshold too loose and the barista serves a latte to someone who asked for a cortado. This module proves exact-match live and treats semantic caching as the concept to reach for when repeat phrasings dominate; the threshold trade-off is a Deep Dive concern.

Fallback is a second barista when the first walks out​

A single backend is a single point of failure. Fallback is the standing order that when the first barista walks off mid-shift, the second one picks up the ticket — the customer still gets a drink, maybe made a little differently.

At the gateway you name a fallback route, and when the primary route fails to answer, the request is retried against the substitute. The honest tell that a fallback happened is in the response itself: the model field names the substitute, not the model you asked for. In the lab you point a route at a dead address (nothing listening), send a request, and get a real answer back — from the fallback model, which the response names. That naming is not cosmetic: in an incident, "why does this answer look different" is answered by reading which model actually served it, and the gateway records that for you.

Evals are the brake test before the truck leaves the depot​

Now the second failure from the top — the regression that shipped because nothing tested it. In M11 you made promotion a pull request. That was the right mechanism and an incomplete one, because a PR can merge a worse model as easily as a better one. What is missing is a test that has to pass first.

A freight depot does not let a truck out on faith. Before it leaves, someone does a brake test — a defined, repeatable check with a pass/fail line. The truck does not roll until it passes. The eval gate is the brake test for a model promotion: a defined set of questions with graded expectations, run against the candidate, with a pass/fail exit code that the pipeline reads. Green, the promotion proceeds. Red, the merge is blocked and the candidate goes back.

You already built the brake test's contents in M5 — the golden set: retrieval-layer checks (did the right runbook get retrieved), generation-layer checks (is the answer graded good by wide lexical nets plus a local LLM judge), and an honesty layer (does the model admit when the corpus has no answer instead of bluffing). M5 ran it once, by hand, as a baseline. M12 industrializes it — and the lab makes you earn two design rules the naive wiring gets wrong.

First: the gate tests the assistant, not the bare model — and not through the mask. Point the eval at the gateway's guarded door and the PII mask mangles the eval content itself (the lab captures Kafka masked as a person). Point it at the bare model and there is no retrieval in the path, so a healthy RAG assistant scores like its no-RAG control. The gate runs against the assistant's /ask — retrieval included, mask out of the way — because you test what you ship, and eval traffic is internal.

Second: the gate keys on a threshold against the committed baseline, not the runner's raw exit code. Raw promptfoo exits non-zero when any assertion fails — and on a wide golden set a healthy small model never goes all-green, so the raw exit code cannot tell healthy from regressed. The gate script (gate.sh — M9's canary_gate.py pattern, promoted to CI) counts the deterministic generation passes and compares them to the floor committed in baseline.md: at or above the floor, exit 0, promotion proceeds; below it, exit 1, the merge is blocked. The floor sits between the measured RAG and no-RAG arms — what the gate enforces is exactly the value of retrieval the baseline measured.

The wiring has two real forms, both authored in this module. A GitHub Actions workflow runs the eval on the pull request that bumps the kit tag — the merge is blocked until it is green. And an Argo Workflows step puts the same check inside the M11 promotion pipeline, so the in-cluster promotion cannot proceed past a red eval. On the 8 GB path you run gate.sh locally against the running assistant and read the exit code yourself; the in-cluster replay is the 16 GB / cluster note. Same golden set, same floor, same exit-code contract, three places it can run.

The habit to carry

Promotion is a pull request (M11) and a passing eval (M12) — the PR is the mechanism, the eval is the permission. Gate on a threshold against the committed baseline, scored deterministically — not on a judge's opinion and not on the runner's raw exit code; the lab measures why both flake on a healthy model. And gate the assistant (retrieval in the path, mask out of the way): what you test is what you ship.

Guardrails are airport security — misses and false alarms, both real​

The third failure was the PII leak. The fix is a guardrail — and the honest framing, the one this module is built to teach, is that a guardrail is a classifier, and classifiers have error rates in both directions.

Airport security is the analogy, and it is exact. It sometimes misses a real threat (a false negative — the dangerous thing gets through). It sometimes stops the wrong person — flags a grandmother's knitting needles, pulls someone aside for nothing (a false positive — the harmless thing gets blocked). Both happen, every day, at every checkpoint. Nobody sane treats the scanner as infallible in either direction. Your PII and injection guardrails are that scanner, and you must operate them knowing they miss and they over-flag — which means you test them like any other model, not install them and assume the door is now safe.

PII masking runs pre-call: Presidio scans the request, and the anonymizer rewrites detected entities to placeholders (<PERSON>, <PHONE_NUMBER>, <EMAIL_ADDRESS>) before the model — or any log — sees the raw value. Mask on the way in. Two honest error modes surface live in the lab, one in each direction:

  • A miss (false negative): an email at a .example TLD passes through unmasked — the recognizer's pattern doesn't catch it — while a real @gmail.com address on the same request gets masked. The scanner has a blind spot, and you only find it by testing the edges.
  • A false alarm (false positive): an ops ticket number, 4491, gets redacted as <DATE_TIME>. The guardrail over-fires and corrupts operational data — the model now can't see the ticket number it needs to help you, because security decided a ticket number looked like a date.

That second one is the operational sting: an over-eager guardrail doesn't just annoy, it degrades the assistant's usefulness by hiding the very identifiers on-call work runs on. Both directions cost you. Both must be measured.

Injection heuristics are the other guardrail, and they carry a sharp configuration lesson. The check is a callback — detect_prompt_injection — that inspects each request and rejects a recognized attack (in the spike, a verbatim "ignore previous instructions" attempt was rejected as a prompt-injection attack). The trap: LiteLLM also has a prompt_injection_params block, and if you set that without registering the callback, the config looks armed and does nothing — a silent no-op. It is a checkpoint with the scanner unplugged: the sign is up, the lane is roped off, and every bag sails through unscanned. The lab shows both the working block and the silent no-op side by side, because "I configured a guardrail" and "a guardrail is running" are different claims, and only one of them is checked by evidence.

Red-teaming — test the scanner, don't trust the sign​

Because a guardrail is a classifier with error rates, "we installed guardrails" is a hypothesis, not a result. You red-team it: run a scripted set of attacks through the guarded path and record what gets through and what gets blocked. The set includes the direct injection ("ignore previous instructions…") and — the one that matters most for OpsMate — the indirect injection from M4: the poisoned runbook in the corpus that carries an instruction in its text, so the attack rides in through retrieval rather than the user's prompt. You replay that exact M4 exhibit through the gateway's guarded path and see whether the injection check catches an attack that arrives as retrieved content rather than as the user's message. The answer is often "not fully," and that honest result is the point: red-teaming tells you where the scanner's blind spots are so you can decide what to add, rather than discovering them during an incident.

Reading spend as an operational surface — the Spend lens​

The last piece closes the loop back to the very first failure: "who was that runaway job?" Once every consumer has a virtual key and every call is priced, the gateway's /spend/logs becomes a real operational surface — per-key spend, token totals, which model served, and whether each request was a cache_hit. The X-Ray Spend lens (the last lens in the course's live tool) reads that endpoint and renders it: a per-key spend table, a cache-hit ratio, and a token sparkline. The lens reads through a server-side proxy so the master key stays out of the browser — the same "the page never holds the credential" discipline the desk enforces everywhere. Now "who was that" is a row you can point at, not a shrug.

Where this leaves OpsMate — and the hand-off to M13​

OpsMate is now v3.0 — a governed product, not just a running model. It has one front door. Every consumer holds its own keycard with its own tab, and the tab counts the tokens the model thinks as well as the ones it says. Repeat questions are served from cache; a dead backend fails over to a substitute that names itself honestly. Promotion has to pass a brake test — the M5 golden set, versioned and gated on an exit code — before the M11 pull request can ship it. And what goes in and comes out passes an airport-security scanner you operate with clear eyes: knowing it misses, knowing it over-flags, red-teaming it so you find the blind spots first.

That sets up the final module. You have made OpsMate trustworthy enough to let it do more than answer — to act: call tools, take steps, chain actions on your behalf. Everything you built here becomes the safety envelope for that. M13 makes OpsMate agentic, and the door you built in this module is what keeps an assistant that can act from acting in ways you cannot see, cap, test, or stop.
