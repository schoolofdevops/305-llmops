Tool-Calling Agents, MCP & the Capstone

Time

Lesson video ~45 min · Lab ~2 hrs hands-on across both parts, depending on your machine.

OpsMate answers questions. During an incident, that is one step short of what you actually want.

Picture the page you got last week: payments-api is crashlooping in the shop namespace. You open OpsMate and ask why. Today it retrieves the crashloop runbook and tells you, correctly, the general procedure for diagnosing a crashloop — check the pod status, read the previous container's logs, look for the failing dependency. All true. All generic. You still have to go run those commands yourself, read the output yourself, and connect the runbook's advice to this pod's actual failure. The assistant knows the playbook; it cannot see the field.

The gap is not knowledge. It is reach. An assistant that can only read runbooks is a colleague on the phone talking you through it; an assistant that can call a read-only cluster inspector is a colleague looking at the same terminal you are. This module closes that gap — carefully. You will give OpsMate the ability to act: to call a tool, look at live cluster state, and ground its diagnosis in what is actually happening right now. And because an assistant that can act can act in ways you cannot see, cap, or stop, everything you built in M12 — the gateway, the virtual keys, the spend accounting, the guardrails — becomes the safety envelope that makes acting safe. This is OpsMate v3.5: a governed, tool-using agent.

Then, in the second half, the capstone: you replay the entire course — synthesize, fine-tune, package, sign, gate, promote, serve — end to end, one take, and assemble the portfolio artifact that proves you can operate an LLM in production.

Resource path — 8 GB core runs the whole module

The agentic half runs two heavyweight tenants at once, and they were measured to co-reside: the M12 gateway stack (LiteLLM + Postgres + Presidio, ~1.9 GiB) plus a slim KIND cluster whose only workload is one crashlooping pod. Together with host Ollama that leaves ~4.1 GiB free on the 8 GB path — it fits, but it is the tightest co-residency in the course, so the lab brings the cluster up small (one node, one broken pod, no in-cluster model server). The capstone half runs on the KIND phase you already know from M8–M11, against the registry data that has persisted the whole time. Distributed tracing of the agent (Phoenix-style span capture) stays a 16 GB note: here, the agent's cost and steps are read from the gateway's spend log and the M12 Spend lens, which is enough to see every tool-call turn.

This is the last module, so it does something the others did not: it points past itself. Agentic systems are a field, not a feature, and doing them properly — multiple agents, sandboxed execution, agent-specific evals, long-running memory — is the subject of the 303-agentops course. This module gives you the bridge: enough of the real mechanism to operate a single tool-using agent on the platform you built, and a clear map of what you deliberately did not cover so you know where the edge is.

The progression teams actually follow​

Start with the map, because "agentic" is a word people use to mean four different things, and the four are a ladder you climb one level at a time.

Naive RAG is where OpsMate has lived since M4: every question triggers a retrieval, whether it needs one or not. Ask it "what is 2 plus 2" and it still embeds the query and searches the vector store — wasted work, because retrieval is unconditional. It has no judgment about when to look something up.

Agentic RAG is the first level up, and the shift is small but real: retrieval becomes a tool the model can choose to call. The model reads the question first and decides — this needs the runbooks, that one I can answer directly. Same vector store, same corpus; the difference is a routing decision in front of it. You do not build this level in the lab (it is one system-prompt-and-a-tool away from where you already are), but it is the conceptual hinge: once retrieval is "a tool the model decides to call," so is everything else.

Tool-using agent — this module — is the level where the tools act on the world instead of just fetching text. A read-only Kubernetes inspector is a tool. So is "restart this deployment" or "open a PR" (which you will not wire, on purpose — read-only is the discipline). The model decides which tool to call, you execute it, feed the result back, and it grounds its answer in real state. This is where "assistant" becomes "agent."

Multi-agent is the top level and the 303 course: agents that call other agents, plan multi-step work, keep memory across a task, and need their own class of evals and sandboxing. Naming it as the top of a ladder whose outline you can already see is the honest way to hand you off — not "here is a magic frontier," but "here is the next level, and here is why it is a course and not a lesson."

The rest of this module is level three, built on one primitive you have to get right first.

Structured outputs — the reliability primitive underneath all of it​

Before any tool call, the thing that makes agents possible at all: getting the model to emit structured output you can act on programmatically, instead of prose you have to parse.

Here is the analogy, and it is the one to carry through the whole module. A junior engineer with a checklist is far more reliable than one you ask an open question. "Go figure out what is wrong with payments" invites a paragraph of speculation. Hand them a form — namespace: ___, pod: ___, what did you check: ___ — and you get back exactly the fields you can act on, every time, in the same form. The form constrains the answer into something the next step can consume. Structured outputs are that form for a language model.

Mechanically, you give the model a JSON schema and require the response to conform to it. Instead of "the pod is probably failing to reach its database, you should check the connection string," you get {"namespace": "shop", "pod": "payments-api", "diagnosis": "db_unreachable"} — a structure your code can branch on. Two flavors of this matter:

  • Structured outputs proper: constrain the whole response to a schema. Use it when the model's job is to fill in a form — extract fields, classify, return a decision object.
  • Tool / function schemas: constrain the model to emit a call — a tool name plus arguments matching that tool's input schema. This is the same machinery pointed at a different target: the "form" is now "which function, with which arguments."

Why this is the reliability primitive and not a convenience: an agent is a loop of model decides → code executes → model reads result → repeat. Every arrow in that loop is code reading the model's output. If the output is free prose, every arrow is a fragile parser one rephrasing away from breaking. If the output is schema-constrained JSON, every arrow is json.loads. The agent is only as reliable as the least structured hop in its loop — which is why structured outputs come first, before tool calling, before MCP, before anything. Get the structure right and the rest is plumbing. (The Deep Dive walks how a schema actually binds generation — constrained decoding — because "the model just returns valid JSON" hides a real mechanism.)

Tool calling on the OpenAI API — how the model "decides," and what it costs you​

Tool calling is structured output pointed at actions. You describe your tools to the model as function schemas; the model, on a given turn, can either answer in prose or emit a tool call — a function name and JSON arguments — which your code executes, feeding the result back for the next turn.

The mechanic worth internalizing: the model does not "run" anything. It emits a request to run something, and your orchestrator decides whether and how to honor it. On the OpenAI-compatible API this shows up as finish_reason: "tool_calls" and a tool_calls array on the message. Your code reads that array, runs the actual function, and appends the result as a role: "tool" message. The model reads that on its next turn and either calls another tool or writes the final answer. Even a 0.6B model does this competently — in the lab you will watch the small OpsMate model pick the right tool with the right arguments off a two-tool menu. Tool calling is not a big-model-only capability; the reliability primitive is real at this size.

Now the ops implications, because this is an operations course and tool calling introduces three costs chat never had:

  • Latency stacks. A chat turn is one model call. An agent turn is model call → tool execution → model call → maybe another. Each hop adds its own latency, and they add up serially. An agent that answers in three tool-calls is at least four model round-trips plus three tool executions before the user sees anything.
  • Loops are a live failure mode. If the model keeps deciding to call a tool, it keeps calling it — and nothing stops it but your orchestrator. This is not hypothetical; it is the single most important thing this module teaches, and you will trigger it on purpose. The phone-menu analogy is exact: you call support, "press 1 for billing," you press 1, and it plays the same menu again. You are stuck not because the menu is broken but because nothing advances you past it. An agent whose tools are still on offer when it should be answering is a caller stuck in that loop.
  • Cost is per turn, and every turn is billed. In M12 you learned that a self-hosted model bills for tokens you never see (the hidden reasoning). Agents multiply that: every tool-call turn is a billed turn, thinking tokens included. A three-tool-call answer is four billed turns, not one. The good news, which you will use in the lab: those turns show up as rows in the gateway's spend log, so the M12 Spend lens is already your agent-cost dashboard — no new tooling.

The loop failure and its fix are the backbone of the lab, so hold this: the reason a loop happens is that the tool is still in the model's action space. As long as the tool schemas are in the request, calling one is a legal move, and a model that thinks it needs more data will keep making that move. The fix is not a clever prompt — it is the orchestrator's job to remove the tools from the turn once the data is in hand. Terminate the loop by taking away the option to loop. (There is a tempting near-miss — tool_choice: "none", which should tell the model "don't call tools this turn" — and in the lab you will watch it get silently dropped on the gateway path, an M2-style compatibility-drift echo at the gateway tier. Omitting the tools entirely is the fix that survives translation.)

MCP in 30 minutes — the USB-C of tools​

You have a tool. How do you describe it to a model — and to the next model, and the model in the 303 course, without rewriting the glue each time? That is the problem the Model Context Protocol (MCP) solves.

The analogy is in the name people give it: MCP is the USB-C port for tools. Before USB-C, every device had its own connector — one cable for the phone, another for the camera, a third for the laptop. USB-C is one physical standard: any compliant device plugs into any compliant port, and the port does not need to know what is on the other end. MCP is that for tools and models. A tool exposes itself through the MCP standard once; any MCP-aware client can use it without knowing how the tool is implemented. Write the Kubernetes inspector once, and OpsMate can use it, the 303 agent can use it, a third-party client can use it — one plug, any tool.

Mechanically MCP is smaller than its reputation. It is JSON-RPC 2.0 over a transport — and the transport you will use is stdio: the client launches the server as a subprocess and they exchange one JSON message per line over stdin/stdout. Three methods carry the whole interaction:

  • initialize — handshake; the server names itself and its capabilities.
  • tools/list — the server returns its tools, each as {name, description, inputSchema}.
  • tools/call — the client asks the server to run a named tool with arguments; the server returns the result.

That is the entire protocol you need for this module. The "USB-C" beat — the one mechanical thing that makes it click — is the conversion in the middle: MCP describes a tool as {name, description, inputSchema}, and the OpenAI API wants {type: "function", function: {name, description, parameters}}. Converting one to the other is a rename of three fields. That tiny adapter is the whole "any tool, one plug" trick: once you can turn any MCP tool schema into an OpenAI tool schema, any MCP server becomes callable by any OpenAI-compatible model. In the lab you will drive the MCP server by hand first — typing JSON-RPC lines and watching the responses — so you see the protocol before you see the agent, then let the agent do the conversion for you.

Servers as containers is the concept note to file away for production. Your inspector runs as a stdio subprocess in the lab, which is perfect for learning and fine for a local tool. In production, MCP servers are typically packaged and run as containers — the same "ship it as an image, run it anywhere" discipline this whole course is built on — so a tool is a versioned, isolated, deployable artifact rather than a script on someone's laptop. You will not containerize the inspector here (the stdio version teaches the protocol cleanly), but the pattern is: an MCP server is just another service you build, sign, and run.

The guarded agent — why M12 is the safety envelope​

Here is the request path you will build, and reading the order is the lesson:

Every model call in that diagram goes through the M12 gateway, and that is the entire safety story. An agent that can act is exactly the thing you do not want holding a master key, spending without a cap, or acting where you cannot see it. Three M12 controls do specific work here:

  • The gateway meters every turn. Each tool-call turn is a billed row in /spend/logs, so the agent's cost — which is higher than chat because it is multiple turns — is visible and attributable to a virtual key, not a shrug. Agent cost is real cost; the Spend lens makes it a number you can point at.
  • Read-only tooling is the visitor badge. This is the M8 callback: a visitor badge opens the doors you need to see and none of the doors you could break. The MCP inspector has exactly two tools — get_pod_status and get_pod_logs — and no mutating verb exists in the server at all. Not "disabled by config" — absent. The agent literally cannot delete, patch, or scale anything, because the capability was never built. That is tool design as a security boundary: the safest way to guarantee an agent cannot do harm is to not give it the ability. An agent that can only look is an agent you can let loose during an incident.
  • The guardrails still run. The injection and PII checks from M12 sit in the same path. An agent reading tool output that contains a poisoned instruction (indirect injection, the M4 exhibit) is a real attack surface — and the door is still the door.

The spend visibility deserves one more beat because it reframes a number you already have. In M12, /spend/logs answered "who was that runaway job?". For an agent, the same log answers "how expensive is this agent, and is it looping?" — a tool that should answer in two turns but shows fifteen spend rows for one question is a loop you can see in the ledger. The Spend lens is the agent-cost dashboard for free. You did not build new observability for agents; you built it in M10–M12 and it already covers this.

The capstone — the full ladder, one take​

The second half of the module is the whole course, replayed end to end, with no new mechanism — every step you have already validated, now run as one continuous lifecycle. A new runbook arrives (you will author a small, realistic one — a cache-eviction incident), and OpsMate's model gets a new version, v1.2, the honest way:

The driving test analogy is the right frame for what this is. All course long you practiced segments on a closed course — parallel parking here, merging there, the three-point turn in its own lesson. The capstone is the examiner in the passenger seat and the full route in one drive: no new skills, but you have to chain the ones you have without stalling. Synthesize from the corpus, tune under the smoke profile, merge and convert to a GGUF, pack and sign as 1.2.0, run it through gate.sh — and the gate must hold the floor — then bump the tag with a PR, let Argo sync it, and confirm it is serving with the dashboards green. The gate is the load-bearing beat: it is entirely possible your smoke-profile v1.2 does not beat the baseline (M6's tuned model didn't), and if it doesn't, the honest outcome is BLOCK — the capstone demonstrates the lifecycle works, which includes refusing a regression, not that every candidate ships.

Then the portfolio artifact: a public repo you assemble from the platform you built — the compose files, the K8s manifests, the Argo apps, a Grafana dashboard JSON, the CI eval-gate workflow, a signed-ModelKit reference by digest, and a written eval report. This is the marketable deliverable, the thing that answers "show me you can operate an LLM in production" with evidence. The lab ships a skeleton and a rubric; you fill it with your run. (It is not auto-published — a public repo is yours to publish, and the course does not push it for you.) The two are not the same thing: the portfolio is the evidence you show an interviewer, and the Udemy completion certificate, issued automatically the moment you finish the course, is the milestone that marks you reached the end.

Course wrap — the skill map, and where the edge is​

OpsMate is v3.5, and the arc is complete. Trace it back: a local model you could barely trust (M1–M3), grounded on your runbooks with RAG (M4–M5), fine-tuned on your domain (M6), packaged as a signed, versioned artifact (M7), served in production on Kubernetes (M8–M9), observable and autoscaled and GitOps-promoted (M10–M11), fronted by a governed gateway with evals and guardrails (M12), and now able to act through read-only tools inside that same safety envelope (M13). That is the LLMOps engineer's skill map — not "prompt a model" but operate one: version it, measure it, serve it, watch it, cap it, gate it, and let it act only where you can see and stop it.

What you deliberately did not cover is the honest edge, and it is the 303-agentops course: multiple agents coordinating, sandboxing for agents whose tools do mutate the world, agent-specific evals (a chat eval grades an answer; an agent eval grades a trajectory — did it take sensible steps, or blunder to a lucky result), and memory operations for agents that persist state across a long task. This module's single read-only agent is the bridge to that — enough of the real mechanism to know the terrain, with a clear marker at the edge of the map. Your certificate closes this course; the next step is where it points.

The lab is where all of this becomes real: you will drive the MCP protocol by hand, watch a 0.6B model tool-call, trigger the agent loop and fix it, then run the full lifecycle one take and assemble the portfolio piece. The Deep Dive goes under the hood of the failure modes — why loops happen at the token level, what the gateway forwards versus eats, and how a schema actually binds generation.

Thirteen modules, one platform, built and measured end to end — thank you for going the distance. If the course earned it, a rating helps the next engineer decide this is worth their thirteen modules. And the honest ask that matters more: put the portfolio somewhere a hiring manager can read it, because that is the artifact that turns what you just built into the next role.
