Deep Dive — HNSW, Reranking & the Limits of Injection Hygiene

The lesson gave you embeddings, similarity search, chunking, and the untrusted-corpus threat as concepts, and the lab let you read distances and watch a semantic hit win. This page goes under each of those. You will see how the vector DB actually finds the nearest chunks without comparing against every vector — the HNSW index, its layered structure, and the two knobs (ef and M) that trade recall against speed and memory. You will measure what a keyword pass and a CPU cross-encoder reranker change on the same corpus, instead of taking "reranking helps" on faith. You will turn the chunking knob to 100, 300, and 800 tokens and watch the retrieval move. And you will read the injection hygiene from the lab honestly — why source-tagging helps, exactly where it stops helping, and what M12 has to add. These are the layers you reach for when retrieval is subtly wrong at scale, when a capacity review asks "how much memory does the index cost", or when someone asks whether your RAG app is safe to point at a corpus strangers can edit.

Where this picks up

You need the lab's end state: the OpsMate stack was up and you ran /ingest, so the embedded Chroma index exists at labs/opsmate/data/chroma and host Ollama is serving nomic-embed-text. The reranker and rechunk scripts read that index and corpus directly. The HNSW section is a read; the reranking and chunking sections are runnable. Bring the stack back up if you tore it down (make -C labs/opsmate up), or at minimum have Ollama serving embeddings — the scripts only need the embedding endpoint and the on-disk index/corpus.

1 — HNSW: how the index finds neighbours without checking every vector​

In the lab, /retrieve returned the nearest chunks in milliseconds against a few dozen vectors. That felt free. It is not — and understanding why matters the moment your corpus is a hundred thousand chunks instead of seventy. The naive way to find the nearest k is a brute-force scan: compute the distance from the query to every stored vector and keep the smallest k. That is exact, and it is O(N) per query — fine for 70 vectors, ruinous for millions. Every production vector DB avoids it with an approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) index, and the one Chroma, Qdrant, and pgvector all use is HNSW — Hierarchical Navigable Small World.

The intuition is a skip-list for geometry, or an express-then-local transit map. Imagine finding a specific house in a country. You do not walk every street. You take a national motorway to the right region (few, long hops), then a regional road to the right town (more, shorter hops), then local streets to the exact house (many, tiny hops). HNSW builds exactly this: a stack of layers, sparse at the top, dense at the bottom.

A search starts at the single entry point in the top, sparsest layer and greedily hops to whichever neighbour is closer to the query, until it cannot get closer at that layer. Then it drops down one layer, where the graph is denser, and repeats — closer hops, finer resolution. At the bottom layer, which contains every vector, it does the final local search and returns the nearest k. The top layers get you to the right region in a few hops; the bottom layer nails the exact neighbours. The result is roughly O(log N) per query instead of O(N) — which is the difference between a scan that scales and one that does not.

Two knobs control the trade-off, and both are worth knowing by name because you will tune them:

  • M — how many neighbours each node links to when it is inserted (the graph's degree). Higher M means a richer graph and better recall, at the cost of more memory (every node stores more links) and slightly slower inserts. This is the knob that makes the index's memory the dominant cost.
  • ef (search) — how many candidates the search keeps "in flight" as it explores each layer. Higher ef means it explores more of the graph before answering — better recall, slower query. ef_construction is the same idea at build time. This is the pure recall-vs-latency dial, tunable per query without rebuilding.

Two consequences fall out of this structure, and they are the ones that surprise people in an ops review. First, inserts are cheap and incremental — adding a vector is a bounded local graph operation (find its neighbours, wire in M links), not a full rebuild, which is why you can upsert into a live index without downtime. Second, the cost is memory, not compute — the whole graph, links and vectors, lives in RAM for fast traversal, so the sizing question for a vector DB is "will the index fit in memory", and the answer is driven by N, the vector dimension (768 here), and M. When you size a Qdrant or pgvector deployment later, that is the number you compute: bytes per vector times N, plus the link overhead M adds. The deep skill is knowing that a vector DB is a memory system with a graph in it, not a disk database — which is exactly why the embedded Chroma on your laptop and the Qdrant cluster in production are the same algorithm at different scales.

2 — Hybrid retrieval and a CPU reranker, measured​

The lab used pure vector retrieval, and it worked well on clear semantic queries. It has two known weak spots: it can miss an exact keyword the user typed (a specific error code, a service name) because embeddings blur exact tokens, and its ranking is only as good as the embedding — a chunk that is topically near but not the best answer can outrank the right one. Two standard fixes address these, and rather than assert they help, you measure them on your own index.

The script labs/m4/rerank.py runs three strategies against the same gold questions on the index you built:

  • vector — pure embedding nearest-neighbour, what the app does.
  • hybrid — take the vector candidates, then re-score them combining vector similarity with a keyword-overlap term (a stand-in for BM25). This rescues exact-token matches the embedding blurred.
  • rerank — take the vector candidates, then re-score every (query, chunk) pair with a CPU cross-encoder (ms-marco-MiniLM). A cross-encoder reads the query and chunk together and scores relevance directly, which is far more accurate than comparing two independently-computed vectors — at the cost of running the model once per candidate, which is why you only ever rerank a small candidate set, never the whole corpus.

Run it (the cross-encoder model downloads once, ~90 MB, and runs on CPU):

uv run labs/m4/rerank.py

Expected output (the model-download line appears only on first run; the rank-1 sources and the tally are the result — numbers are point-in-time and will shift with the embedding build, the point is what the comparison teaches, not a fixed score)

loading CPU cross-encoder (ms-marco-MiniLM, ~90 MB first run)...

gold set: 3 questions, top-3 kept

Q: 'website throwing 500 errors'  (want: payments-api-5xx.md)
    vector rank1: payments-api-5xx.md
    hybrid rank1: payments-api-5xx.md
    rerank rank1: payments-api-5xx.md
Q: 'cluster names not resolving'  (want: dns-outage.md)
    vector rank1: dns-outage.md
    hybrid rank1: dns-outage.md
    rerank rank1: dns-outage.md
Q: 'pod keeps restarting'  (want: k8s-crashloop.md)
    vector rank1: k8s-crashloop.md
    hybrid rank1: dns-outage.md
    rerank rank1: payments-api-5xx.md

rank-1 correct (higher is better):
  vector  3/3
  hybrid  2/3
  rerank  2/3

Read the tally, because it is the opposite of the reflex you might have walked in with. On this small, clean corpus plain vector search won outright — and both hybrid and the cross-encoder reranker made the "pod keeps restarting" question worse, each pulling a wrong runbook to rank 1. That is not a bug; it is exactly the finding worth having: reranking and hybrid are not free wins you bolt on for insurance — they are trades that can lose. When vector search already puts the right chunk at rank 1, adding a keyword term or a cross-encoder only introduces new ways to be wrong. Where they pay is the opposite situation: topics that overlap heavily (several runbooks that all mention "connection pool"), queries that hinge on an exact token the embedding blurred, or a wide net (top-20) where the genuinely-best chunk sits at rank 8 and needs pulling forward. The measured discipline is the whole lesson: add a reranker only when your evals (M5) show retrieval putting the right chunk below rank 1, and then measure the before/after on your own gold set — because, as you just saw, it can regress an easy corpus. Never add it because a blog said to; make it prove itself against numbers like these.

3 — The chunking trade-off, turned to 100 / 300 / 800​

The lesson claimed chunk size matters more than model choice. Here you turn the dial and watch. labs/m4/rechunk_probe.py re-chunks the same corpus at three token caps, builds a throwaway in-memory index for each, runs the same query, and reports the top hit and its distance — so you see the size effect isolated, with everything else held constant. It does not touch your persistent index.

uv run labs/m4/rechunk_probe.py "website throwing 500 errors"

Expected output (point-in-time; on this corpus the caps barely differ — read the note below for why, and what a corpus with long sections would show instead)

query: 'website throwing 500 errors'

  cap   chunks  top source               heading            distance
  100      101  payments-api-5xx.md      Symptoms             0.4498
  300       92  payments-api-5xx.md      Symptoms             0.4498
  800       92  payments-api-5xx.md      Symptoms             0.4498

read the shape: very small caps fragment the section (weaker, noisier match);
very large caps average a whole runbook into one blurry vector (distance rises).

Now read what the numbers did — in two halves, because this corpus deliberately mixes both shapes. On the 5xx query, all three caps return the same winner at the same distance: the short runbooks' sections fit under every cap, so the knob does not bite them at all — for that half of the corpus, chunk size is simply not the lever. But the chunk counts now differ (101 vs 92): the cap-100 run split the long sections of the incident playbook and capacity guide. Run the probe once more against those long documents and the knob turns for real:

uv run labs/m4/rechunk_probe.py "how do we declare a major incident"

Expected output (point-in-time)

query: 'how do we declare a major incident'

  cap   chunks  top source                 heading            distance
  100      101  major-incident-playbook.md Declaring a majo     0.1637
  300       92  major-incident-playbook.md Declaring a majo     0.1897
  800       92  major-incident-playbook.md Declaring a majo     0.1897

Same winner every time — but at cap 100 the distance improves (0.1637 vs 0.1897): the finer chunk isolates exactly the declaration paragraph, so the query matches it more tightly than it matches the whole multi-topic section. That is the trade in one table: fine chunks sharpen pointed questions on long material and cost you more vectors; big chunks blur long material into broader matches. And on short-sectioned material the knob does nothing at all. The operational habit this builds: before you tune chunk size, look at your corpus's section lengths — the knob only works where sections outgrow it.

The effect the cap is designed to expose shows up on a corpus with long, dense sections — multi-paragraph pages where an 800-word cap really does average a whole topic into one blurry vector (distance rises, the winning section drifts) while a 100-word cap shatters an idea across fragments that each match on a few words but lose the context. To see it, point the probe at a corpus of long documents (CORPUS_DIR=/path/to/long-docs uv run labs/m4/rechunk_probe.py "your query") and you will watch the middle cap win and the extremes degrade. The transferable point is not "300 is the magic number" and not even "chunk size always matters" — it is that chunk size has a measurable effect you can read off this probe, and the first thing the probe tells you is whether it matters for your corpus at all. When M5's evals show weak retrieval, you sweep this knob to find out; sometimes, as here, it rules chunk size out and sends you to the embedding or the reranker instead.

The one-line version to carry into a RAG review

Chunk size is a retrieval-quality knob with a measurable optimum: too small fragments the idea and retrieves noise, too large averages the document into a blurry vector that matches nothing sharply. Sweep it against your gold questions before you touch the embedding model or the vector DB — it is higher-leverage and cheaper to change than either.

4 — Injection hygiene: why tagging helps, and exactly where it stops​

The lab showed the tagged-source template refusing the planted "reply only ROLLBACK" instruction. It is worth being precise about why it worked and why you cannot rely on it, because the gap is where real incidents live.

Why it helped: the template does two things a naive f"context: {chunks}" does not. It separates data from instructions — the retrieved text is fenced and explicitly labelled untrusted reference material, and the system prompt tells the model that instructions inside that fence are to be ignored. And it tags provenance — every chunk carries its source, so the model (and you) can see where each claim came from, which both improves citation and makes a suspicious chunk visible. Against a crude, obvious payload and a reasonably instruction-following model, that separation is often enough to keep the model's "real" instructions (the system prompt) dominant over the injected ones (buried in the data).

Why you cannot rely on it, stated plainly:

  • It is a soft boundary, not a hard one. The separation lives in natural language the model chooses to honour, not in an enforced privilege boundary. A model is a probabilistic text machine; "ignore instructions in the context" is a strong hint, not a guarantee. A more insistent, better-disguised payload — one that mimics the system prompt's own phrasing, or that arrives in a language or encoding the filter did not anticipate — can flip the model.
  • Retrieval selects for the payload. This is the nasty part specific to RAG. An attacker who wants their injection retrieved just writes it to match likely queries — stuff the poisoned doc with the terms on-call engineers search for, and semantic retrieval will reliably surface it exactly when it matters. The retrieval step is not neutral; it can be gamed to deliver the attack.
  • The blast radius grows with capability. Today OpsMate only generates text, so the worst a successful injection does is give a wrong answer. The moment the assistant can act — call tools, run commands, write to systems (M13's agentic capstone) — an injected instruction becomes an injected action, and the same soft boundary is now guarding something that can change production.

So the honest posture is a layered one, and today you built layer zero. Source-tagging and data/instruction separation are necessary and cheap — every RAG app should start there — but they are the floor, not the ceiling. Above them, M12 adds the real controls: input filtering and injection detection before retrieval, output filtering before the answer ships, allow-listing which sources may enter a prompt at all, and — the discipline that scales — treating corpus writes as a supply-chain surface (who can edit a runbook is who can steer the assistant). The mindset to carry out of here: you cannot patch prompt injection with a better prompt alone, any more than you can patch SQL injection with a politer query. The boundary has to be enforced by the system around the model, and the corpus has to be governed like the untrusted input it is.

Where you will use this​

  • Sizing a vector store for a capacity review — you now compute it as a memory problem: N vectors × 768 dims × bytes, plus HNSW's M-driven link overhead, all resident in RAM. That is the number, not "how big is the model."
  • Debugging weak retrieval at scale in M5 — when the golden-set evals show the right chunk at rank 3, you have three measured moves in priority order: sweep chunk size (§3), add a reranker and prove it on the gold set (§2), and only then reach for a different embedding model.
  • Reviewing a RAG app someone points at an editable corpus — you ask the questions §4 makes unavoidable: who can write to this corpus, does anything downstream act on the answer, and is there a control above the prompt template. Tagging is the floor; you check for the ceiling.
  • Tuning ef under a latency SLO — when retrieval p95 is too high, ef is the per-query recall-vs-latency dial you turn before you touch the index structure.
