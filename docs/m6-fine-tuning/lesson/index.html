Fine-Tuning — Synthetic Data, LoRA & QLoRA

Here is the situation this module is built for. OpsMate has retrieval now. Ask it about the payments runbook and it pulls the right pages and answers from them. But your team keeps flagging the same thing in review: the answers do not sound like your runbooks. Your runbooks say "check the db_pool_in_use gauge and cancel the blocking backend"; OpsMate says "you may want to investigate whether database connections are being held open for an extended period." Both are correct. Only one reads like it came from your on-call channel. Retrieval put the right facts in front of the model, but the model still phrases them in generic-assistant voice, ignores the format your team expects, and buries the one command someone needs during an incident under three sentences of preamble.

That is the wall this module is about. You already fixed the knowledge problem in M4 and M5 — RAG gets the right runbook in front of the model. What is left is a behaviour problem: the model's voice, format, and defaults are not yours, and no amount of retrieval changes them, because retrieval feeds the model context — it does not change how the model writes. When the problem is behaviour, not knowledge, the tool is fine-tuning — you adjust the model itself so its defaults become your defaults.

But fine-tuning is the expensive, slow, easy-to-fool-yourself option, and reaching for it when a cheaper tool would do is one of the most common mistakes teams make. So the first thing this module gives you is not a training script — it is a decision. When is tuning actually the answer, and when is it a costly way to solve a problem a prompt edit or a retrieval fix would have handled for free?

When do you tune? The decision ladder​

Reach for the cheapest tool that solves the problem, and only climb when it genuinely cannot. There are three rungs, cheapest first.

Read the ladder as a set of honest questions you ask in order:

  • Can a prompt edit fix it? If the problem is "it does not cite the runbook" or "it is too verbose" or "it should refuse questions outside the corpus" — that is a prompt problem. You proved this in M5: swapping system.txt for system-v2.txt moved the scores with zero code change. Never tune for something a prompt line would have fixed. This is the rung most misdiagnosed "we need to fine-tune" requests actually belong on.
  • Is it a knowledge gap? Use RAG. If the model gives generic advice because it has never seen your runbooks, the fix is to show it your runbooks — retrieval, which you built in M4 and measured in M5. Fine-tuning facts into a model is a bad way to teach it facts: it is expensive, it goes stale the moment a runbook changes, and it cannot cite a source. RAG is how you do knowledge.
  • Is it a behaviour gap that survives with no prompt and no context? Now tune. OpsMate's problem is the third kind: even with the right pages retrieved and a careful prompt, the voice and format are wrong. Tuning bakes the behaviour into the weights so the model defaults to it — your phrasing, your structure, your "command first, explanation second" — whether or not RAG is attached. That is what no prompt and no retrieval can buy you.

The honest cost/benefit: tuning costs you a data-building effort, a training run, and a new artifact to version and serve — and it does not replace RAG. A tuned model still does not know a runbook you wrote yesterday. The realistic end state is both: RAG for the knowledge, tuning for the voice. This module builds the tuning half and then measures it honestly against the RAG baseline you recorded in M5 — because the only way to know tuning helped is to weigh it on the same scale.

LoRA — renovate with removable fittings, don't rebuild the walls​

The naive picture of fine-tuning is "retrain the model", and that picture is what makes people think it needs a data-center. Qwen3-0.6B has about 597 million parameters; full fine-tuning updates every one of them, which needs the memory to hold the whole model plus its gradients plus the optimizer state — far more than a laptop has. If that were the only way to tune, this module could not exist on the 8 GB path.

It is not the only way. The technique that changes the economics is LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation — and the analogy is a tenant renovating a rented building.

You are renting. You cannot knock down walls or touch the structure — the landlord froze all of that (these are the model's frozen weights). But you can add removable fittings: shelving, partitions, fixtures you bolt on alongside the existing structure. You install a small set of your own fittings, you use them, and when you move out you can either leave them in or unbolt them and carry them to the next place. LoRA is exactly this. The base model's weights stay frozen; you attach a small, low-rank adapter — a thin extra path alongside the frozen layers — and you train only the adapter. The building is untouched; you only ever paid for the fittings.

How small is "small"? Here is the real number from OpsMate's tune, and it is the number to remember from this whole module:

trainable params: 1,146,880 || all params: 597,196,800 || trainable%: 0.1920

You train 0.19% of the model. About 1.15 million parameters out of 597 million. Everything else is frozen. That is why LoRA fits on a CPU laptop: you only hold gradients and optimizer state for the 0.19%, not the whole model. And it is why the resulting adapter is tiny — a few megabytes on disk, next to a base model of hundreds of megabytes. Two consequences fall straight out of that, and both matter later in the course:

  • Adapters are swappable. Because the adapter is a small separate thing bolted onto a frozen base, you can keep several — one tuned for OpsMate's SRE voice, another for a different team's — and load whichever you need onto the same base. The fittings come off.
  • Adapters are cheap to ship. A 5 MB adapter is a trivial artifact to version, sign, and package. M7 does exactly this: the adapter (or the merged model it produces) becomes OpsMate v1.0, a versioned model artifact alongside the v0 base. The tiny footprint is what makes that packaging story practical.

The "low-rank" part is where the small number comes from, and you only need the intuition, not the linear algebra. A full weight update to a layer is a big dense matrix. LoRA approximates that update as the product of two much thinner matrices — a tall-skinny one and a short-wide one — whose shared inner dimension is the rank, r. A small rank (this lab uses r=8) means those two matrices are tiny, so the number of trainable parameters is tiny, while still being enough to nudge the layer's behaviour. You pick which layers get an adapter, too — this lab adapts the attention query and value projections (q_proj, v_proj), the common minimal choice. More rank and more targeted layers buy more capacity to learn (and more room to overfit); the Deep Dive measures that trade-off with a real trainable-parameter table.

QLoRA — the same idea, one tier up (a single beat)​

You will hear "QLoRA" constantly, so here is the one-beat version. QLoRA is LoRA with one addition: the frozen base is stored quantized — 4-bit instead of 16-bit — while the LoRA adapter still trains in higher precision on top. In the renovation picture: the building itself is kept in compressed storage while you renovate, so it takes a quarter of the space, and your fittings are unaffected.

Why it exists: a 4-bit base is a quarter of the memory, so QLoRA is what lets a much bigger model — a 7B or 13B — fit on a single small GPU that fp16 could never hold. That is the whole point of the "Q": it is a memory trick for the frozen part, so you can tune large models on modest hardware.

When you would reach for it: when the base you want to tune is too big to fit otherwise. On this course's 0.6B model it is overkill — the base already fits in full precision on CPU — which is exactly why the lab's load-bearing path is plain LoRA on CPU, and QLoRA is an optional GPU enrichment track (a Colab notebook in the lab folder). Know the word, know when it matters, and do not reach for a GPU-and-quantization stack to tune a model that fits on your laptop as-is.

Synthetic data — build the practice question bank​

An adapter is only as good as what you train it on, and the honest bottleneck of fine-tuning is almost never the training — it is the data. To teach OpsMate your voice and format you need examples of questions with answers written the way you want them. Where do a few hundred of those come from? You are not going to hand-write them.

The analogy is how you actually study for an exam. You have the textbook (your corpus — the runbooks). You do not re-read the textbook to prepare; you build a practice question bank from it — you turn each chapter into questions and work them. Synthetic data generation is that, automated: you walk the corpus and, for each section, ask a model to write a few question/answer pairs grounded in that section. The runbooks are the textbook; the generated Q&A pairs are the practice questions you drill the model on.

The pipeline is three steps — generate, filter, split — and the middle one is where the quality lives:

The rule that governs the whole thing is garbage in, garbage out. A model that generates its own training data will happily produce malformed pairs, questions with no question mark, answers that drift into generic advice the section never mentioned, and near-duplicates. If you train on that garbage, you get a garbage adapter — and worse, you will not know, because the loss will still go down. So the filter is not optional cleanup; it is the part that decides whether the data is worth training on. OpsMate's filter throws out pairs that are too short or too long, that are malformed, that duplicate another question, or whose answer shares no vocabulary with the section it was supposedly grounded in — and it logs every rejection with its reason so you can look at the garbage you kept out. In the lab you will read three generated samples including one the filter rejected, because seeing the reject is how you build the instinct for what "good data" means.

The one rule you cannot break: no contamination​

There is a discipline here that is easy to get wrong and expensive when you do. The M5 golden set is your test set — the yardstick M6 must beat. Your training data and your test data must never overlap. If a question you trained on shows up in the eval, the model has effectively seen the answer key, and every number after that is inflated. Training on your test set is the oldest way to fool yourself in machine learning, and it is silent — the scores look great and mean nothing.

So OpsMate honours a promise M5 made and keeps a hard line at the same time. The promise: the golden set's questions were authored from corpus runbooks, and M6 reuses those same runbooks as the domain map for generating data. The line: the synthesizer reads only the corpus, never golden.yaml. The synthetic questions come from the same sources as the golden set (the same runbooks) but are freshly generated, different questions — and the golden set stays completely untouched, held out, so the measurement at the end is honest. Same sources, different questions, test set held out. That is the contamination boundary, and the lab enforces it in code.

Reading a loss curve — what the training tells you while it runs​

When you train, the one number that streams out is the loss — a measure of how wrong the model's predictions are on your training data, step by step. You built the vocabulary for this back in M1 (the model predicts the next token; the loss measures how surprised it was by the real one). During tuning you watch the loss to read what is happening, and there are two shapes to know apart.

A healthy curve falls and then flattens: the loss drops as the adapter learns your data, then levels off as there is less left to learn. That flattening is your signal that more steps are buying little. The spike run showed exactly this shape — loss falling from about 5.05 toward 3.54 over a handful of steps — the model visibly learning the data.

The shape to be suspicious of is loss that keeps dropping toward zero on a tiny dataset. That is not always good news: on a small set, a model can overfit — memorize the exact training examples rather than learn the general behaviour. A memorized model aces its training data and generalizes badly to the real questions you will actually ask. On a deliberately tiny run you can watch overfitting happen (the loss goes suspiciously low), which is instructive — but for a model you intend to use, you want it to generalize, which is why the lab trains a small number of epochs and then stops, rather than driving the loss into the floor. The loss curve is a live readout of that trade-off, and the X-Ray Train lens plots it for you as it streams.

Merge, or serve with the adapter attached?​

Once the adapter is trained you have a choice, and it is worth understanding because it comes back in M7.

  • Merge. Fold the adapter's learned changes back into the base weights, producing one normal, standalone model with no adapter attached. The upside: it serves exactly like any other model — llama-server does not need to know LoRA ever existed, so nothing about your serving stack changes. The downside: the fittings are now built in; you cannot unbolt this adapter and swap a different one without re-merging from the base. This is the choice OpsMate makes, because the goal is a servable tuned model that drops into the existing stack.
  • Serve base + adapter separately. Keep the base and the adapter as two artifacts and have the serving runtime load the adapter at serve time. The upside: the adapter stays swappable — one base, many adapters, switched at load. The downside: your serving runtime has to support adapter loading, which not every engine does.

For OpsMate, merge wins: you want a single GGUF the M3 llama-server serves with only a filename change. So the lab merges, then converts the merged model to a q8_0 GGUF (the same quantization vocabulary from M2, the same convert path from M3) and drops it beside the base GGUF. The serving stack is untouched — you swap which file the model service loads, and that is the entire deployment of a model you trained yourself.

The measurement ritual — weigh on the same scale, read the result honestly​

This is the climax of the module, and it is a discipline more than a technique. You have a tuned model. Is it better? The trap is that a tuned model always feels better — you built it, you wanted it to work, and it will produce a few answers that delight you. Feelings are not evidence.

The analogy is weighing yourself before and after a diet. The claim "I lost weight" means nothing unless you weighed before and after on the same scale. Change the scale and the number is meaningless. Your golden set is that scale, and M5's baseline.md is the "before" weight, recorded and committed precisely so this comparison is real and not a memory. Now you put the tuned model on the same scale — the same golden set, the same graders — and read the number.

Three things about doing it right:

  • The retrieval layer is a control. Tuning changes the model, not the index. So the retrieval score should be unchanged (24/24 before, 24/24 after). If it moved, something other than your tune changed, and you have a bug to find before you trust any other number. A control that stays put is how you know the rest of the comparison is clean.
  • Add a no-RAG, tuned-direct arm. The most interesting question about a tuned model is what it can do on its own, with no retrieval — because that is where tuning, not RAG, is doing the work. So you run the tuned model with no context and compare it to the base model with no context (M5's no-RAG arm). That gap is the value tuning added, isolated from retrieval.
  • The honesty rule: beat, tie, or lose, every outcome teaches. A tuned 0.6B model trained on a few hundred synthetic samples might beat the RAG baseline, might tie it, or might lose to it — and you report whichever happened, straight. If it beats: tuning bought you real behaviour improvement, measured. If it ties: no regression, which is itself worth knowing — you can ship the voice you wanted at no cost to quality. If it loses: that is the most honest lesson of all — tuning is not an automatic win, thin data or a tiny model has limits, and RAG and tuning answer different problems. RAG answers knowledge problems; tuning answers behaviour problems. A tune that "loses" on a knowledge-heavy golden set may still have perfectly fixed the voice problem you tuned it for — the golden set just is not measuring voice. Reading the number honestly, and understanding what it does and does not measure, is the skill. The lab writes the prose for all three outcomes; the validation run folds in whichever one actually happened on the real stack.

That last point closes the loop the whole course has been building. M4 taught you retrieval. M5 taught you to measure it and recorded the baseline. M6 tunes the model and holds it to that baseline — and the answer, whatever it is, is defensible because it was weighed on the same committed scale. That is the difference between "the tuned model feels better" and "the tuned model scores X on the golden set, here is the file."

What you'll do next​

In the lab you will bring the stack down first — training wants the RAM the stack was using, so the one-stack-at-a-time rule now applies to training itself. You will synthesize a few hundred grounded Q&A pairs from the corpus and inspect the good and the rejected. You will run a real LoRA fine-tune — 0.19% trainable, a few minutes on CPU — and watch the loss curve stream through the X-Ray Train lens. You will merge the adapter, convert the merged model to a GGUF, and swap it into the compose model service with a single env var. Then the measurement: re-run the M5 golden set against the tuned model, fill your baseline-mine.md M6 rows, and read the result with the honesty rule. Teardown keeps the adapter, the tuned GGUF, and the training logs — because M7 packages them into OpsMate v1.0.

The skill you leave with is the whole loop: decide whether to tune, build the data, run the tune, and — the part most people skip — measure it honestly against a recorded baseline instead of shipping on the feeling that it got better.
