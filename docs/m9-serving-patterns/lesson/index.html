Serving Patterns — Canary a Model on Quality, Not Crashes

Here is the situation this module is built for. A new version of OpsMate's model comes out of M6 — it was fine-tuned, it passed its checks in staging, and someone wants it shipped. In M8 you served exactly one version behind a Service; now there are two versions and one production. The obvious move is to swap the tag and roll it out. But that bets every user on a model that has only ever been measured in staging, and production traffic is not staging traffic — it is broader, messier, and it asks questions your held-out set never did. So the real question is: how do you ship a model version without betting every request on it at once?

And there is a trap waiting inside that question, one that catches people who are fluent with Kubernetes rollouts. When you roll out a normal service, "the pod is 1/1 Running" is a good enough proxy for "it works" — a web API that starts and answers /health is almost certainly serving correctly. A model server breaks that assumption. The pod can be 1/1 Running, the readiness probe green, every request answered with a 200 and a clean usage block — and the model can be wrong: giving worse answers, hallucinating, regressing on exactly the cases you fine-tuned it to improve. The pod is healthy while the model is bad, and a readiness probe cannot taste the food. It checks that the kitchen is open, not that the cooking is any good.

By the end of this module you will have run two model versions side by side behind one router, sent a thin slice of real traffic to the new one, and let your golden set — the same yardstick that set the M5 baseline and rejected this exact tune in M6 — decide its fate in production traffic too. You will promote or roll back on data, not on the absence of a crash. This is OpsMate v2.0 growing a safe way to change what it serves.

The new cook shadowing — one table in ten, and the manager tastes the food​

Before the mechanics, the picture to hold in your head. A restaurant hires a new cook. No sane kitchen puts them on every order on day one. Instead the new cook shadows: they cook for one table in ten while the trusted cook handles the rest. That much is the canary you already know from ordinary deploys — a small slice of live traffic to the new thing, so a problem hits few people, not everyone.

But here is the part that is specific to models, and it is the whole module. How does the kitchen decide whether the new cook is good? The weak way is to wait for complaints — send out the plates and see if anyone sends food back. That catches disasters (a raw chicken, a pod that crashes), but it misses the quiet failures: food that is edible but worse, that a polite customer eats without a word and never returns for. The strong way is that the manager tastes the food — actively, before it reaches the customer, comparing the new cook's dish against the trusted cook's on the same order.

Map that onto serving a model:

  • One table in ten = routing ~10% of requests to the new model version (the canary). Blast radius bounded.
  • Waiting for complaints = watching for crashes, 500s, CrashLoopBackOff. Necessary, but it only catches the model falling over, never the model being worse.
  • The manager tasting the food = running your evals — your golden set — against the canary's answers and comparing them to the base. This is the only thing that catches a healthy pod serving a bad model.

The manager tasting the food is your golden set. The crash signal and the quality signal are different signals, and for a model the one that matters most is the one a probe cannot see.

Analogy, stated plainly

Canary = the new cook shadowing: one table in ten, and the manager tastes the food (evals) rather than waiting for complaints (crashes). Traffic splitting bounds the damage; only tasting — evals — tells you whether the new version is actually any good.

The router and the split — how two versions live behind one address​

Mechanically, running two versions at once needs three things: two backends and something in front that decides, per request, which backend answers. In M8 you had one backend (opsmate-model) and clients dialed its Service directly. Now you add a second backend (opsmate-canary, serving the M6 candidate) and put a router in front of both. Clients dial the router; the router splits.

The router here is one small nginx pod using split_clients — a built-in that hashes a per-request key across the 0–100% space and assigns each request to an upstream by weight buckets. Two lines of config carry the weights: 10% canary, everything else base. Over many requests the distribution converges to those weights. And every response carries an X-Served-By header naming which backend answered — the per-request observability hook that lets you watch the split happen instead of trusting it.

Two design choices matter here and both are deliberate:

  • The client does not change. Both backends serve the same OpenAI /v1 contract under the same model alias (qwen3-0.6b), so the client cannot tell which one answered and does not need to. The endpoint contract that held from laptop to cluster now holds across two versions.
  • Changing the weight is a manual, visible act. Edit the two lines in the ConfigMap, then kubectl rollout restart so nginx re-reads it. Manual on purpose — you feel the promote/rollback in your hands before this module shows you the abstractions (KServe, the Gateway API) that automate it. An abstraction you adopt before you have felt the mechanic is a black box; one you adopt after is a convenience.

Reading a split honestly — you cannot judge a coin from three flips​

Here is a trap that looks like a bug and is not. You set the canary to 10%, send 20 requests through the router, count the X-Served-By headers — and see zero canary hits. Is the split broken? No. The probability of a 10% canary getting no hits in 20 independent requests is 0.9²⁰ ≈ 12% — about one time in eight, you will see zero, purely by chance. Nothing is wrong; 20 is simply too few requests to read a 10% split.

This is judging a coin from three flips. Flip a fair coin three times and getting three heads (or three tails) is common — it tells you almost nothing about whether the coin is fair. You need many flips before the observed ratio means anything. Same here: at n=100 the same 90/10 split lands around 92/8 — close enough to the weights that the split is legible. The discipline is: read a split at a sample size that can actually show it. A canary you evaluate on 20 requests is a canary you are guessing about.

This is not a detour — it is a canary lesson in miniature. The whole point of a canary is measuring the new version, and measuring needs enough samples to be real. Twenty requests lie the same way a three-flip coin lies.

The quality gate — the yardstick that rejected it in M6 gets to kill it in production​

This is the climax of the module, so hold it clearly before the lab: the golden set gets to kill the canary.

Recall the thread. In M5 you built a golden set — retrieval tripwires plus graded generation questions with wide contains-any nets — and it set OpsMate's baseline (the number every later stage must beat). In M6 you fine-tuned the model, ran that same set against it, and it lost: the tuned model's generation regressed below baseline, so it was not shipped — kept in the registry as 1.0.0-candidate, honest history. That candidate is exactly the model your canary now serves.

So the quality gate in this module is not a new idea — it is the M5/M6 yardstick, pointed at production traffic. canary_gate.py runs the golden generation questions against the canary backend and the base backend directly, scores each with the golden set's own wide nets, and compares pass-counts. The candidate must at least tie the base to earn promotion — it is replacing a model that already works, so "no worse" is the floor. And because it is the M6 loser, it does not clear that floor. The gate prints ROLLBACK.

Read what this changes. Promotion and rollback stop being crash verbs — "it fell over, roll it back" — and become data verbs: "the golden set says it is worse, roll it back," or "the golden set says it holds, promote it." That is the difference the module exists to teach. The readiness probe told you the canary was healthy. The golden set told you it was wrong. You act on the second.

The habit to carry

Before you promote a model version, run your eval set against it AND against the version it would replace, and compare. "The pod is Running" is a crash signal; "it scores at least as well on the golden set" is the quality signal — and for a model rollout, the quality signal is the one that decides. If you cannot measure the candidate against the incumbent, you are not canarying, you are hoping.

Staged promotion — the new driver does not get the school van on day one​

Rollback is one direction; promotion is the other, and it is not a single jump either. When a candidate does pass the gate, you do not slam it to 100%. You climb a ladder: 10% → measure → 50% → measure → 100%, and you can pull it back a rung the moment the gate goes red.

Think of a new driver at a delivery company that also runs the school van. You do not hand them the school van on their first day. Weeks on the delivery van first (10% — low stakes, cargo not children), then a light passenger route (50%), and only after they have held up under real conditions do they get the school run (100%). At every rung the blast radius is bounded, and you can demote them instantly if a report comes in. Staged promotion is that ladder for a model: each rung is gated on the golden set, and each rung limits how many users a bad version could reach before the gate catches it.

The mechanic is exactly the weight edit you already learned: change the split_clients percentages, rollout restart, re-run the gate. In the lab you will drive the ROLLBACK (the candidate lost), then drill the promote ladder upward using the base as a stand-in, so you have felt both directions.

When the router is not enough — KServe, and the frontier​

The nginx router is the cheapest honest canary: one 64Mi pod and a ConfigMap you can read end to end. It is right for OpsMate on a laptop. But it is coarse — it splits per request by a blind hash, it cannot scale a model to zero, and re-weighting is a manual edit. When those limits start to bite, you climb the ladder. Two rungs are worth knowing by shape, even though this course surveys them rather than installing them on 8 GB.

KServe turns serving into a declarative CRD, the InferenceService. The two weight lines you edit in an nginx ConfigMap become a single field, canaryTrafficPercent, and the controller runs both revisions, splits by that percent, and can scale either to zero:

That is a real upgrade — declared, reconciled, scale-to-zero — but be honest about the cost: KServe rides on Knative Serving, a networking layer, and cert-manager, and those are dependencies you install, run, and keep patched. On an 8 GB single node that stack outweighs the one split you need, which is why the lab reads the InferenceService YAML rather than applying it (the live path is a 16 GB+ cluster and a recorded demo). The rule is the ladder: adopt KServe when a fleet of models and scale-to-zero justify its dependency stack, not before.

The frontier — llm-d and the Gateway API Inference Extension. At real scale, the routing decision itself wants to be inference-aware, and that is beyond anything nginx can do. Two ideas define it. First, the Gateway API Inference Extension introduces an InferencePool of model-server pods with an endpoint-picker that routes by KV-cache and prompt-prefix affinity — sending a request to the pod that already holds its prompt's cache, not to a random one. Second, disaggregation: M1's two phases — prefill (read the prompt, build the KV cache, compute-bound) and decode (emit tokens off that cache, bandwidth-bound) — become two separate pools, each sized and scaled for its own bottleneck. That call-back to M1 is the whole point: the phases you first met as a memory diagram become, at scale, two fleets with a cache-aware router between them. You do not need to run this to read it; the goal is fluency, so when you meet an InferencePool in a design doc you know what it buys and what it costs.

The decision tree — the one artifact to keep​

Four patterns, one choice each time you serve a model: plain Deployment + Service (M8, one version), router + canary (M9, two versions on a laptop), KServe (a fleet with scale-to-zero), llm-d / Gateway API Inference Extension (cache-aware routing at scale). They form a ladder of capability against a ladder of dependency cost, and you climb only as far as your problem forces you. You will build this into a one-page serving decision tree (labs/opsmate/k8s/DECISION-TREE.md) — the capstone artifact this module leaves behind, referenced again in M13.

Where this leaves OpsMate — and the seam to M10​

OpsMate can now change what it serves safely: two versions behind a router, a golden-set gate that promotes or rolls back on data, and a decision tree for when to climb past the router. But notice what the gate depended on — you could only kill that canary because you could measure it. The quality signal came from evals you ran deliberately; the traffic split came from a header you read. That is the hinge into M10: you cannot canary what you cannot observe. Next module wires real observability — traces, metrics, the signals that let you see a regression in production the moment it starts, not only when you go looking with a golden set.
